Community practice · Family support

Poom Family: Coaching That Begins at Home

A home visit can reveal the everyday context behind a young person’s goals. The Poom Family homecare coaching project brought child and caregiver support into that context, in partnership with a community welfare center.

01 / The setting

For children growing up amid economic strain or changing family circumstances, schoolwork is only part of the story. Study routines, relationships, and confidence take shape at home. Working with SkyDream Coaching Psychology Center and a community welfare center in Gimpo, I joined a project serving upper-elementary and middle-school youth from multicultural, single-parent, and economically vulnerable families.

The welfare center helped select families and connect them with practical community resources, including support for everyday needs. Our psychology team visited homes to offer child coaching, psychological assessment and interpretation, caregiver coaching, and family activities. The project also included parent education sessions.

A goal becomes more reachable when the people and routines around it are part of the conversation.
02 / The work

Listening to the whole family

In my role as a counselor and program evaluator, I looked at how a child’s motivation, peer relationships, and daily routines related to the caregiver’s perspective and the home environment. Coaching sessions translated broad hopes into smaller goals that a young person and their caregiver could discuss and revisit together.

The team used assessment to inform conversations with families and examined pre–post program data internally. These observations helped us reflect on the service; they do not establish a causal effect or stand in for each family’s experience.

03 / Beyond the visit

Community support is part of the picture

Home coaching could address conversations and habits within a family, while the welfare center helped connect families to resources beyond the visit. The experience sharpened my interest in how family, community, and school support can work together. It also made a practical gap visible: the project had no direct school partnership, so changes at home were not consistently connected to classroom support.

In the classroom · May 2025

The Next Level of Learning

At Hwanggok Elementary School, I designed and led a four-session academic motivation program for a third-grade class. We approached the question “How do I learn?” through a game-like journey, with characters, cards, and choices that students could make their own.

A learning journey

Make the invisible visible

Motivation can sound abstract to a child. I developed a story world called I’m on the Next Level in Education to give students a way to talk about their learning style, the energy behind their goals, and the habits or feelings that get in the way. Each session turned an idea into something they could see, hold, draw, or try out.

01 / Discover

Meet your learning character

Students watched a custom animation and explored DISC-inspired animal characters. A character badge offered a playful starting point for naming strengths, possible blind spots, and ways of working with others.

02 / Aim

Find your energy and a goal

Activities about study energy and learning needs led students toward a goal they could recognize as their own. Collecting energy stickers made the idea of motivation concrete.

03 / Notice

Name the Black Mamba

A deck of “Black Mamba” cards turned common obstacles to studying into characters. Students identified the barriers that felt familiar, then drew their own and put words to the thoughts behind them.

04 / Respond

Choose a strategy, take a step

“Cloud and Fire” solution cards helped students match obstacles with study strategies. A shield, helmet, and sword game led into a symbolic final act: stepping on the Black Mamba they had drawn and celebrating with a high-five.

From framework to classroom

Turning learning competencies into play

I developed the four-session curriculum by drawing on the component domains of the official learning competency assessment jointly developed by the CNC Cognitive-Behavioral Research Institute and the Strength Coaching Research Institute. I connected those domains with DISC personality characteristics, then designed a story and character activities that children could explore in the classroom.

The badges and illustrated cards below show how the program translated learning strengths, everyday obstacles, and practical study strategies into hands-on activities.

In practice

More than a lesson about trying harder

The aim was to help students recognize that a difficulty in learning can be named and approached with a specific strategy. Designing the activities and leading them in the classroom brought together my interests in motivation, self-directed learning, and early support for students who may be starting to disengage.
