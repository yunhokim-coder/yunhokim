Master’s Thesis

Kim, Y. (2024). A Study on the Effectiveness of a Grit-Enhancement Group Coaching Program for Upper Elementary School Students. Master’s thesis, Kwangwoon University.

Abstract

This thesis evaluated a seven-session group coaching program based on a tripartite model of grit that includes situational adaptability. Twenty-four upper-elementary students participated in a quasi-experimental design: eight received the tripartite-grit program, eight received a two-factor grit comparison program, and eight formed a no-program control group. Grit and self-directed learning were measured before the program, immediately afterward, and at a two-week follow-up. The tripartite-grit group showed a significantly greater increase in grit than the comparison and control groups. Effects on self-directed learning were not statistically significant. The findings support the program’s potential for strengthening grit in this sample.

Undergraduate research paper · 2019

Development of the Happiness Habit Scale

Kim, Y., Kim, C., Kang, C., & Kang, M. (2019). Development of the Happiness Habit Scale [Departmental research paper]. Research in Industrial and Organizational Psychology, 26, 150–158. Kwangwoon University, Department of Industrial Psychology.

Overview

Beginning with a 26-item pool, the study analyzed data from 163 young adults using item analysis, internal-consistency analysis, and exploratory factor analysis in SPSS 20.0, resulting in a 15-item, three-factor measure: Agency, Positive Perception of Adversity, and Interpersonal Relationships. The initial 26-item measure showed Cronbach’s α = .808, and criterion-related validity was supported by a significant positive association with the Satisfaction With Life Scale (r = .399, p < .001).

Scholarly product · 2025

Memorial Night: Pet Loss Syndrome

Kim, Y. (2025). Memorial Night: Pet Loss Syndrome (추모의 밤: 펫로스 증후군) [eBook]. ISBN 979-11-982644-0-4.

Overview

This psychoeducational eBook supports people grieving the loss of a companion animal. Drawing on psychological literature and interviews with people who have experienced pet loss, it explores common emotional responses and the ways attachment can shape grief. The guide discusses coping strategies, memorial practices, and approaches to recovery in accessible language. Developed as part of a pet-loss support project, it is intended for bereaved pet owners as well as counselors, educators, and others who support them.

Selected presentations · February 2024

From a Coaching Program to the Conference Stage

At the Korean Coaching Psychology Association’s winter conference, I presented my master’s research on a group coaching program designed to strengthen grit among upper-elementary students.

The question

Can grit be practiced?

My research began with a practical question: how can a child keep working toward a goal while adapting when circumstances change? The seven-session program treated grit as more than persistence alone. It brought together sustained effort, continued interest, and situational adaptability in activities for upper-elementary students.

The evidence

What the study showed

I presented a three-group study with 24 students and assessments before the program, immediately afterward, and two weeks later. Grit improved significantly more in the program group than in the comparison and no-program groups. The effect on self-directed learning was not statistically significant, a useful reminder that gains in one outcome do not automatically extend to another.

Academic exchange · National Taiwan Normal University · August 2024

Why I Took Grit to Taipei

Taiwan shares Korea’s collectivist roots and its high regard for education, yet it reports far greater well-being. I went to find out what that difference looks like from inside a classroom, and presented my research on grit along the way.

By Yunho Kim · Presenter

While working on my thesis, I read a paper on grit by a doctoral researcher at National Taiwan Normal University (NTNU). Our questions overlapped, so I wrote to the author, introduced my own study, and proposed that our groups meet to share work.

That email became an academic exchange. In August 2024, I joined master’s and doctoral students at NTNU. Each of us presented our research in English, then opened it to discussion and defense-style questioning. A faculty member joined the later part of the session.

Why Taiwan

Same roots, different outcomes

The triarchic model of grit that my thesis built on was developed in Hong Kong, so Hong Kong might have seemed the natural place to go. I chose Taiwan on purpose. Korea, Hong Kong, and Taiwan are all collectivist societies that place enormous value on education. But in international comparisons such as the World Happiness Report, Taiwan sits well above both Korea and Hong Kong. Taiwan also struck me as having a more horizontal culture than Korea.

That contrast raised the question I most wanted to ask. In Korea, grit can become obsessive: persistence driven by pressure rather than by interest or purpose. If Taiwanese students share the same cultural roots, what makes their striving look different?

The presentation

Grit, across cultural contexts

My thesis tested a group coaching program built on the triarchic model, which adds adaptability to situations to the familiar two factors of perseverance and consistent interest. Presenting it in Taiwan, to researchers who think about motivation in a related but different culture, made the cultural argument feel less like a footnote and more like a live question.

Explaining the study in English to an unfamiliar audience also meant stating the design, the effect, and its boundaries plainly, without leaning on shared assumptions. Their questions sent me home with a clearer sense of how to strengthen my next study, whatever its topic.

Different ages, different positions, one table. Ideas were weighed on their merits.
What I observed

Impressions from inside the conversation

These are informal impressions from conversations, not measured findings. They still gave me hypotheses worth carrying forward.

  • More horizontal classrooms.Educators and teachers seemed, on the whole, more horizontal in how they related to students.
  • More room for autonomy.Students’ autonomy appeared to be recognized more readily than I was used to.
  • A gentler intensity.The drive around education felt somewhat less intense than in Korea.
  • Support that creates slack.Well-developed social support systems seemed to leave students with more breathing room.
The room itself

A culture of open questioning

The seminar itself reflected those impressions. Participants differed in age and academic standing, yet the dynamic was noticeably horizontal. Students challenged one another directly and generously. When the professor joined, the conversation did not turn into a lecture.

Listening to their questions, seeing how they framed their own research, and following the way they reasoned widened my own perspective. It made me want to study motivation not only as a trait to be strengthened, but as something shaped by the culture around a student: how much autonomy they are given, how much pressure they carry, and how much support catches them.

The exchange also taught me how scholarship grows. It started because I read someone’s work closely and reached out. I intend to keep doing that.
