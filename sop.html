Statement of Purpose

First Draft

Updated September 2026

Why this work matters

The 12th economy in the world when we started with nothing (virtually no natural resources to speak of while the Korean War destroyed the nation), and with 54.5% of the Koreans educated up to college level as of 2023.

But all these statistics are like hollow victories – because we are not happy. Our happiness index as of 2023 stands lowest among the OECD nations.

In fact, we rank No. 1 in suicide rate, 18 years straight.

Youths are no exception. Their life satisfaction level is also the lowest (6.96 points) and for many “good” reasons; life for the youths, just that of the grown-ups is harsh at best. Performing poorly on the Korean SAT, for instance, can mark you for life, defining your future social status literally speaking. And you get only one chance per year. Likewise, one mistake can be very costly.

In fact, recently the news of cram institutes for preparing students for medical school shocked many, especially since they target elementary school students. This may sound unbelievable, but sadly and unfortunately it is true.

As such, youths experience stress from all sides at a time when they are most sensitive as they are undergoing drastic physical and psychological changes, often accompanied by severe anxiety.

Thus, I always longed for programs that serve the struggling students. Of course, there are some like the Weclass, a counseling project for schools that got jump started in 2008, but as of 2025, only 60% of the schools adopted this program. And even so, the reality today is that there is only one counselor per school, while there are hundreds if not more students.

Therefore, it is not an overstatement to say that we need to develop more of such programs because my goal is to help struggling, especially under-achieving students.

Where my questions began

In many ways, this goal was born because of my own story. To put it simply, I could have easily been recruited for a TV program on problematic children that is very popular today in Korea. I was not an easy child to raise.

But I was lucky. I had many mentors - good teachers from a charter school and my mother, a psychologist.

Thus, I also tried to help the youths recover or better yet, develop preventive measures before their conditions worsen. In many ways, this is why most of the lines on my CV are relevant, volunteering for, coaching and mentoring hundreds if not more youths including North Korean defectors and other struggling youths.

However, as I work with them, the list of pressing questions increases, I realized, and thus, I am now applying for a doctoral program to conduct research on the following.

Motivation and grit

First, I am interested in studying motivation and grit, but for the youths who face a tough environment that offer virtually no “ingredient” for success. And I did meet and work with many such youths. For instance, for a project that I am working on today with a social welfare center, most if not all my subjects are the children and youths of financially challenged families, including those who are North Korean refugees who often find life in the South just as harsh as their former life in the North, marked by poverty, hunger and political oppression. The list goes on; children who are so called halves with a non-Korean mother, for instance, who not only face discrimination (Korea is not exactly an inclusive society; statistics speak out loud), but also low expectation to begin with.

As such, given this dire external condition, how can I ensure that these youths are motivated, self-control, and exert grit when the entire world seems to be against your success.

In fact, I wrote my master’s thesis on grit which did back-up the hypothesis that grit indeed affects self-discipline and self-control, all which are essential for overcoming society’s negative perception and low expectation. In fact, grit also is a predictor for academic success in schools. Grit manifested a positive effect on school adjustment (Kang Ji Sun et.al, 2022; Song Song Min Gyo, 2022; Kim Sun Jung et.al, 2023; Yoon Gi Bong et.al, 2023; Lee Na Young, 2023). As grit improves, emotional intelligence also develops, enabling students to respond flexibly even in negative environments and form positive interpersonal relationships, which in turn positively influences school adjustment (Song Min Gyo, 2022).

And for this, to foster grit, effective programs are urgently called for. In fact, through the Home Care Planner Psychological Coaching Project carried out in collaboration with the Welfare Foundation, early intervention is proven to be crucial for proactively addressing potential maladjustment and deviant behaviors in adolescents who come from financially marginalized family, educational, and psychological environments. This is because it takes several times more time and effort to help maladjusted adolescents recover and grow after the critical adolescence period.

To address this, I implemented psychological coaching, very much like the motivational interviewing that aims to strengthen individuals' psychological capacity so that they can take the lead in their own growth – which demonstrated that intervention programs are effective. For instance, my family’s psychological center is currently collaborating with a major satellite city’s social welfare office and the national fund office to coach the subjects who are experiencing academic anxiety, helplessness, and low motivation.

For the two sessions conducted to this day, I utilized the curriculum, coaching tools (role-playing) and the gamification tools I developed; if nothing else, there is nothing like one-side lectures that will guarantee loss of attention, while virtually everything becomes fun when gamified. But more than anything, I used these means since most of the subjects facing the above-mentioned conditions tend to hide behind silence.

I am also currently collaborating with professors and researchers at a cognitive behavioral research institute to develop the Motivational Leadership Handbook for elementary, middle, and high school students that contains a psychological education program and tools designed to foster self-leadership, enabling students to grow independently.

And as expected, these tools really were very effective indeed and I started to witness changes. Likewise, I wish to conduct in-depth research to develop many more creative, effective coaching tools.

Happiness and culture

My other interest which is connected to grit is the study of happiness, which in Korea is still considered a new less tapped area. As mentioned earlier, South Korea is known for its intense focus on education, and the perseverance and passion students show are remarkable. However, despite high levels of grit, life satisfaction and happiness among students remain paradoxically low.

To explain this, Professor Datu from the University of Hong Kong, a country where a similar pattern exists (strong persistence in education but low happiness levels), suggests that in collectivist cultures—where individuals are highly sensitive to others' expectations, interests, and contextual factors—'contextual adaptation' is essential. He argues that to study grit in a way that positively correlates with happiness, we need to include 'contextual adaptation' as a third subfactor of grit.

To this, I would like to add on the cultural element as well while conducting research at your program because of another interesting study. In Korea, researcher Lee Soo-ran and colleagues found that 38.1% of university students showed signs of 'obsessive grit'—driven by compulsive passion. They argue that this contributes to the low happiness levels observed. Instead, they propose the need for 'harmonious passion' (like contextual adaptation) where individuals pursue activities autonomously and with the flexibility to engage in other areas of life. They suggest that a Korean-style grit scale should incorporate this concept.

However, upon reviewing the Korean grit scale myself, I found that it lacked sufficient coverage of both harmonious passion and contextual adaptation. As a result, I chose to conduct my research using Professor Datu's scale from the University of Hong Kong and was able to obtain more meaningful results compared to the traditional two-factor model. Although still preliminary, I also developed a happiness habits scale as a small thesis project during my undergraduate studies.

I wish to continue to develop this type of measure that factors in the variables that may be culture-specific in your program.

Systems of support

Moreover, I also see the need for a support system network. In 2024 I completed the first cohort of the Korea Instructor program offered by Coalition of Youth Against Drugs (COYAD) and Youth Safety Shield, a youth drug prevention organization, led by the professors and doctoral-level experts in the US. The core curriculum included drug education, adolescent development, clinical psychology, addiction counseling to prevent drug addiction.

However, this type of superb program is yet to take its roots in Korea, perhaps because awareness of drug addition is still low in Korea (most Koreans believe that Korea is a drug-free country which is far from the truth). As my primary focus will be on the adolescents who face a tough reality, studying in the US makes most sense to me because this type of program is not only effective for drug-related issues but also for addressing a wide range of psychological crises in students.

Last, but never the least, I am also interested in counselor training as I experienced the challenges as well. There are times when I feel emotionally drained, but I persisted, thinking about my younger self. But that it me – personal motivation alone may not suffice. Thus, I will need to study their motivation as well but also the comprehensive set of systems and processes needed to ensure their mental health as healthy counselors provide healthy counseling.

As a last note, my conviction is that people can change for the better, and I want to ensure to play a part as a professor/scholar who continues to work with the youths.
